# Episode 13 - Beginnings

**The Narrator:**  
When I was growing up there were still bombsites all across London, some are still there,  but I saw them fresh. Cratered ruins of buildings pushed into the ground, collapsing through their foundations to kiss the blued clay browning in the air. They were wonderful playgrounds. We roamed across them, tossing rubble and watching it stumble down the uneven slope, clicking out stuttered revolutions.  

My favourite was finding metallic shards and weaving stories about their lives before they came to rest down here on the ground. Could it be part of a cast-iron radiator, shivered into fragments as the room exploded? The stifling domestic scenes broken open in a riot of sound and heat. Or the broken shaft of a German cannon split into parts by an ack-ack gun’s tracer reaching out to stroke the plane and pull it lovingly back to earth?  

I found a wing tip once, nestled in a cairn of shattered brick, its curved end crumpled like the discarded newspaper underfoot after a friday’s fish supper. I couldn’t tell which nation it belonged to, just that once it lived in the sky and now it was here, still, clamped down by gravity’s grip.  

We cut ourselves open on the picked over carcass of the city. The sharpened skin and bones of the buildings cracked open and exposed, only magical now we could see them upended and rearranged. There were whole street’s worth of houses pulled from their sockets and mixed in with each other, their rubbled skeletons unsheathed and shining in the rain and sun.  
 
Even though the last bombs landed years ago people were still found buried, their corpses cowered in basements covered over with a capstone of toppled walls. Or torn apart and strewn together, mingled carelessly with shattered cobble and curbstone torn out from the street. It was rare though. Most had been found when their remains were still intact enough to grasp. Picked up and carted away, then placed back in the earth, shrouded in wood and silk, for those that could afford it. I suppose the care kept their ghosts quiet and quiescent.  They were able to rest now their covering was deliberate rather than carelessly tossed on by some impersonal force falling from above.  

*music break*  

There were other places. Places where the graves remained as improvised and violent as the deaths they marked. You felt it through your feet, could almost taste the confusion and amnesiac rage in the mist blanketing the ground after the sun had set. Sometimes there were trees ringing those places. Their trunks hunched, barked jackets roughly furrowed, branches reaching up to scratch the sky. If you ran a finger across the scars you could feel the heated metal still tearing through the air, all those years ago.  

In those days you could still forage in the city. Its ruins offering itself up, a gift to those that still ran in its streets and gave it life. Some of those trees still bore fruit. Every bough garlanded with blossom, white tinged pink and purple leached from the sky when each spring wound around. Then, with the heat of summer, they’d be borne down, hanging heavy with apples or pears or plums, skin glistening in the sun. It was… beautiful.  

Most of it was eaten quickly, snatched by hungry hands almost as fast as it grew. I once saw two men fight over a handful of hard unripe apples but they crushed them in the scuffle. Anything left fed the birds as it fell, slowly dissolved into the earth, folded under then sucked in by the hungry roots of the grass and bramble knotting across the broken ground. For me it felt like a net of souls holding each other gently, the death of one leading to the life of another. It brought me some comfort, later.  

My favourite time was autumn, that’s when the blackberries would appear. Though we rarely got the apples or pears, there were always blackberries, they grew everywhere. All those missing families nourishing deep roots. The berries bunched amongst the catching thorns spread along the bramble’s wiry webbing arms. We were small enough to get to them, scampering paths through the tangle. We loved to stain our mouths purple-black, feasting till our stomachs revolted, casting out the sweet dark fruit spiked with stomach acid. It made no difference to us or the dogs that would follow us around. We’d be laughing and picking more, as the strays would lap at the mounded vomit, their meal mush and half digested, catching clods of earth with every lick, flapping their ears to scare the wasps circling in their angry banded jackets.  

There was more than just the protective barbs you had to be wary of. Unsavory types stalked the ruined city too. Most were hungry for the possessions of those who crossed their path. Others wanted more. They looked to take parts of people, prise away fragments to carry in their pockets, warming themselves with what they’d stolen. Some watched for careless children, whisking them away to sell to lonely couples looking for a family or…worse. They had no luck with us.  

We were only children but that meant we could fit in places no one else could. The scratches on our skin - proud marks of passage; red lines plotting out the map of our progress. Sometimes you’d happen across a stretch of bramble where the berries were soft and full and plump but their colour hadn’t darkened. They sat there ruby red, almost bleeding, aching to be picked. Over time, we learned to let them lie. If we’d watched the birds flocking everywhere but there, leaving only those berries, so ripe and inviting, untouched, perhaps we would have learnt the lesson without the same bite.  

The first time we tried them was on an autumn afternoon bathed in soft golden october sun. It had been a hungry day, all morning our stomachs had grumbled over bread and dripping, barely enough to keep them quiet. And then there they were. Like manna from heaven, an entire bush unplucked. There were four of us - Charlie, Sasha, Crispin and me. We all set about feasting, gorging our mouths red and puckered around the fruit’s tang then sitting down to rest and warm ourselves on seats of powdered masonry. Ever so slowly it crept up on us.  

*music break*  

Slipping around the edges at first, a trembling in the peripherals, suggestions of buildings growing from their splinters climbing like shadows, disappearing as soon as we turned to look. The memories built and thickened; an old woman’s wrinkled hands, a beloved dog, a stolen service pistol, a child I didn’t recognise but knew I would die for. We were adrift watching the city’s history layering in front of us, we saw it all together; carts of plague dead rolling through empty streets to the clangour of doleful bells; buildings of stone built up by sandal shod labourers; fire dancing up walls and leaping from roof to roof; families parading arm and arm, laughing between market stalls; knots of cattle driven in a rush by ruddy faced men and their squat biting dogs.  

It was a palimpsest we couldn’t navigate, trapped in its shifting layers. By the time we regained our senses, the sun was already dipping. But it took till after dusk for our surroundings to settle back into a single shape while we shivered in the cold. It didn’t seem like much at the time, too overwhelming to be held, a strange fever dream we’d shared. None of us spoke of it as we stood and left, parting ways silently, heading home.  

My dreams were haunted that night. I relived memories I’d never had, suffered through heartbreak at the hands of strangers and deep biting grief at the death of parents and lovers I’d never met before. So many tragedies, so many crises, so many joys, I felt every one. It aged me. The next day I was a cracked urn leaking out the confused memories of a tangled multitude, they seeped out behind me like a snail’s shining path. The future and past merged into a confusing mess of unlit passageways, dark cobbled paths with no exits, a labyrinth I couldn’t recognise but had to walk.  

I knew the others had seen what I had, we had shared that journey. We should have spoken, but somehow there weren't the words. That or we couldn’t find them, we didn’t have a language for something like this. So we buried it, put it back where we found it, in the ground, along with our friendships. We never spoke again.  

It passed as the weeks went on, leaking away each night, slowly sloughed off in my dreams. Then one day it was gone. I saw the others on the street or on the bus and could see it had left them too. All but one. That one taste had hooked Charlie, drawn him in and held him there like the barbs on the bush. He always wanted to talk but I just couldn’t do it. I slid away from the greetings till they stopped. Afterwards I saw him now and then ducking into alleys or slinking over rubble piles talking with the memories real for him and the dead. He was found on a November morning huddled up under a bramble bush on the edge of a bombsite, his mouth stained red, his face etched with years he hadn’t lived. Some years ago, I managed to dig out his coroner's report, no easy task. His insides were a mess of desiccation, each organ dried out and shrivelled, his chest hollow with the space between them, any enlivening liquid absent.  

I learnt that year that you should be careful where you go a-picking. Roots drink deep, and who knows what kind of memories they can pull from the ground. Some things are best left buried, hidden from greedy little hands. Remember that the next time you pull a berry from a bush. You can never be sure where its winding roots reach and what kind of fragments are shrouded within those ruby coats waiting for living tongues to taste and draw them in, far too deep.
